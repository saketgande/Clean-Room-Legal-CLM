# Ask narrower questions, let code decide

Aegis's AI is inconsistent mainly because of how its questions are shaped, not because of the model or its settings. Sending the same input again often returns a different answer because the question is open-ended ("find every deviation", "list every obligation") and the answer is long. Wherever Aegis asks a closed question with one short answer, it is already perfectly repeatable. In the 2026-10-28 replays, intake triage, email triage and portfolio filtering were 100% stable. Obligation extraction (same contract: 38, 40, then 41 obligations) and playbook review (1 issue on one run, 3 on another) were the least stable. The research points the same way. Hosted models are not fully repeatable even at temperature 0, the setting meant to make the model always pick its likeliest word, and the variation grows with output length and with how open the question is ([Atil et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04667v5); [Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)). Contract benchmarks show the model's main failure is quietly missing things, not inventing them ([ContractEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03080); [Better Call GPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.16212)). The fix, which matches how Ironclad, Spellbook and A&O Shearman's ContractMatrix describe their products, has four parts. Split each big open task into many small, closed questions over the clauses Aegis's document reader already finds. Make every answer point to exact text that code can check. Let code, not the model, turn answers into decisions. Ask twice where it matters, and send disagreements to a person. Aegis already does this for clause labelling and obligation dates, so most of the needed groundwork exists. The first changes should be playbook review, obligation extraction and the metadata truncation bug.

## Temperature 0 cannot buy repeatability; question shape can

Many people assume that setting temperature to 0 makes an AI model deterministic, meaning the same input always gives the same output. On hosted services it does not, and the reason is now well understood. A provider's servers process many customers' requests together in batches, and the arithmetic comes out very slightly differently depending on how big the batch is. Usually that makes no difference. When the model is almost exactly torn between two next words, though, it tips the choice, and everything written after that point follows a different path. In one experiment, an open model was asked the same question 1,000 times at temperature 0 and gave **80 different completions**. All 1,000 agreed for the first 102 words, then split at word 103 ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)). Full determinism is achievable with special server code, but it runs about 1.6 to 2 times slower, and hosted APIs such as the one Aegis uses do not offer it. OpenAI describes its own reproducibility setting as "best effort" ([OpenAI Cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/reproducible_outputs_with_the_seed_parameter)). Several sources quote Anthropic's API reference as saying temperature 0 results "will not be fully deterministic", but that wording could not be found on the current page ([CAMEL-AI docs](https://docs.camel-ai.org/reference/camel.configs.anthropic_config)). The practical conclusion stands either way: Aegis cannot configure its way to identical answers. It has to be built to expect a spread of answers and to handle that spread on purpose.

What can be controlled is how far the answers spread. A study of five models, each given the same tasks 10 times at temperature 0, found accuracy moving by **up to 15 points between runs**. It also found that **every model was more stable when its answers were shorter** ([Atil et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04667v5)). The same study separates two kinds of agreement. Compared word for word, answers rarely matched. Once each answer was reduced to its actual decision, agreement was far higher: 96.4% against 46% on one task. Microsoft likewise notes that allowing longer outputs makes responses less deterministic ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/foundry-classic/openai/how-to/reproducible-output)). Chaining steps makes things worse, because instability multiplies: four steps that are each 95% stable give about 81% stability end to end ([Atil et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.04667v5)).

Legal judgement adds a further layer. Researchers asked 500 hard legal questions, taken from appeal cases where the judges themselves disagreed, 20 times each at temperature 0. **Claude 3.5 changed which side it backed on 10.6% of questions**, GPT-4o on 43% and Gemini 1.5 on 50%. A legal expert found the reasoning on both sides of these flips sound: the models weighed vague standards differently on different runs ([Blair-Stanek & Van Durme](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05196v1)). That gives two useful lessons. First, Claude was the most stable model tested, so moving to another model is not the answer. Second, when the model flips on a question, that usually means the question is genuinely borderline. A flip is worth showing to a lawyer, not hiding.

Aegis's own measurements follow this pattern closely. The table sorts each task by the shape of the question it asks. The "stable" figure is the share of inputs where all three replays reached identical decisions. "Overlap" measures how much the replays' answers had in common: 1.0 means they matched completely, 0.5 means they shared about half.

| Task | Question shape | Fully stable | Overlap |
|---|---|---|---|
| intake_triage, email_triage, portfolio_filter | One short, closed answer | 100% | — |
| tabular_cell_extraction | One closed answer per cell | 75% | 0.89 |
| contract_edit_suggestions | Edit to a known clause | 75% | 0.92 |
| renewal_extraction | Few fields, one confusable label | 50% | 0.92 |
| contract_metadata_extraction | Many fields in one long answer | 50% | 0.83 (5 of 12 truncated) |
| intake_form_read | Open "what's notable" list | 50% | 0.76 |
| clause_labeling | Many closed answers, one per clause | 0% | 0.88 |

| obligation_extraction | Open "list every obligation" | 25% | 0.49 |

The clause labelling row needs a careful read. Each run makes dozens of separate decisions, one per clause. If even one of them differs, the whole run counts as unstable, so 0% fully stable is what you would expect even when about 88% of labels hold steady. Clause labelling is not the problem. Its design is the right one, and the remaining flips are almost certainly the genuinely ambiguous clauses. The real outliers are the two open "find everything" tasks. Their overlap of about 0.5 means two runs over the same contract share only about half their findings. Earlier research saw the same thing: when an issue-extraction pipeline was run twice on 50 court judgments with identical settings, only **60% of documents came out split into issues the same way** ([arXiv 2607.03325](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.03325)). That figure comes from a search excerpt, because the PDF itself could not be read. For a CLM, the consequence is that a lawyer could open the same contract twice and see a different list of problems each time. That is unacceptable in legal work even if the average accuracy looks fine.

## Contract AI misses things quietly, so coverage must come from code

The benchmark research on contracts gives a consistent picture of how models fail. **They miss clauses more often than they invent them.** ContractEval tested 19 models on a standard set of contracts with expert-marked clauses (CUAD). It found models often answer "no related clause" when one exists, a habit the authors call "laziness". It also found that "thinking" modes, where the model reasons step by step before answering, improved some scores but **reduced correctness** on simpler tasks ([ContractEval](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.03080)). In "Better Call GPT", ten real procurement contracts were reviewed by lawyers and by models. GPT-4 matched junior lawyers at spotting issues (score 0.871 against 0.860) in 4.7 minutes instead of 56. However, the models "favored precision over recall": they rarely flagged something wrong, but they often missed issues by concluding none existed ([Better Call GPT](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.16212)). The senior lawyers who wrote the answer key also agreed with each other only imperfectly (α = 0.719), a reminder that "correct" is itself a judgement call. The Stanford study of legal research tools found that answers can cite a source that does not support the claim, which the authors call misgrounding. Even purpose-built tools got answers wrong or misgrounded in **17% to 33% of queries** ([Magesh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20362)).

Two research findings explain why a single "review the whole contract" request does badly. First, models get less reliable as the input grows. They are best at using information near the start or end of a long input and worse at using what is in the middle ([Lost in the Middle](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172)). When the question and the relevant passage share few exact words, most models tested fell **below half of their short-input accuracy at 32,000 tokens** ([NoLiMa, ICML 2025](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/modarressi25a.html)). Chroma found the same decline across 18 current models, including Claude 4 ([Chroma](https://research.trychroma.com/context-rot)). Second, narrower tasks over less text found more. A non-peer-reviewed thesis on the CUAD contracts saw recall rise when context was reduced and tasks were focused, at some cost in precision ([UFPE](https://repositorio.ufpe.br/handle/123456789/58332?locale=en)). Here recall means the share of real items the model found, and precision means the share of its findings that were real. In plain terms, when the model is asked to hold a whole contract and every rule in mind at once, items in the middle get missed, and which ones get missed changes from run to run. That matches playbook review's 1-versus-3 result exactly.

To be honest about the evidence: **no study compares "one big review prompt" with "one small question per clause or per rule" on contracts and measures repeatability.** The case for splitting is indirect, from long-input research, reasoning research ([least-to-most prompting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10625)) and the contract-extraction thesis. Every one of those sources points the same way, though. Aegis's replay harness can settle the question for Aegis's own contracts.

The design conclusion is that **the list of everything that could be found should come from code, not from the model.** Aegis already has this. The deterministic document reader splits each contract into numbered clauses with exact character positions, and project policy already says the deterministic layer is authoritative. Clause labelling uses it correctly: the model receives each clause's number and a short excerpt and answers "clause 14 → limitation of liability". Code then attaches the exact text. When the model skips a clause, the result is a visibly unlabelled clause, not a gap nobody can see. This is the pattern the research recommends: process the document piece by piece, have the model return clause IDs rather than copying text, and let code check that every piece got exactly one answer ([Anthropic, handling stop reasons](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/handling-stop-reasons)). Obligations and playbook review should be rebuilt on the same base.

## The industry builds playbook review as a checklist, not a hunt

Commercial contract-review tools all describe roughly the same design, though nearly all their accuracy figures are the vendors' own claims. Ironclad turns each playbook into explicit rules. Each rule has a **preferred position** (the wording the company wants), one or more **fallback positions** (acceptable compromises) and a trigger for escalating to a person. Ironclad checks each playbook against a "golden set" of agreements redlined by hand before it goes live, reviews contract by contract and clause by clause, and delivers each redline as a Word tracked change. The user switches each redline between preferred, fallback or none ([Ironclad](https://ironcladapp.com/resources/articles/contract-review-agent-redlining-playbook)). Spellbook attaches the same fields to each rule: fallback, risk level, preferred wording, a suggested comment and a required approver ([Spellbook](https://spellbook.com/learn/spellbook-alternatives)). A&O Shearman's ContractMatrix grounds its suggestions in a bank of approved precedents and returns them as tracked changes so a lawyer always decides ([Harvey/A&O Shearman](https://www.harvey.ai/en-US/customers/a-and-o-shearman)). One practitioner guide adds a test worth adopting: any clause that no playbook rule covers should be **flagged for a person, not passed silently** ([bogumilluc.pl](https://bogumilluc.pl/en/lab/agent-workflows/legal-playbook-review)).

This design plays to what models measurably do well and avoids what they do badly. Spotting an issue against a stated standard is where models come closest to lawyers. Writing the redline itself is the weakest step. In the only independent benchmark, lawyers scored **79.7% on redlining against 65.0% for the best AI tool**, and the tools mostly "inserted standard text instead of making nuanced edits" ([Vals VLAIR](https://www.vals.ai/vlair)). The same benchmark found AI answers were usually entirely right or entirely wrong, with little partial credit, so checking each item matters more than any overall score. Taking replacement wording from the playbook's pre-approved language, rather than having the model draft freely, uses the model where it is strongest. Aegis's playbook review today does the opposite. It is a single open "find every deviation" request over the whole contract, so the model must decide which rules apply, find the clauses, judge them and decide how many issues to report, all in one long answer. That combines all three causes of instability: a long input, a long output and an open-ended task.

The rebuilt version works like a checklist. For each playbook rule, code picks the clauses that could be relevant, using the clause labels Aegis already stores, and asks one closed question. The model answers with a fixed choice, such as *meets preferred*, *meets fallback*, *deviates* or *not addressed*, plus the exact quote it relied on and a one-line reason. Code then checks that the quote really exists in the contract text. Code also decides what each answer means: "deviates" on a high-risk rule becomes an issue, and "not addressed" on a required rule becomes a missing-clause issue. Finally, code flags any clause no rule looked at. Anthropic calls this pattern sectioning, meaning independent sub-questions run side by side, and recommends fixed workflows in code over open-ended model-led processes whenever the steps are known in advance ([Anthropic, Building effective agents](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). A playbook is by definition a known set of steps. The number of issues then comes from counting rule verdicts, so it cannot drift from 1 to 3. Only a verdict on a borderline clause can change, and that change is a signal worth showing to a lawyer.

## Ask small, check in code, vote where it matters

Several techniques reduce or contain the run-to-run spread, and each has evidence behind it.

**Closed answers with structured output.** Anthropic's structured outputs feature constrains the model so its answer always fits a defined format, for example a fixed list of allowed labels and required fields ([Anthropic docs](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs)). This guarantees the *form* of the answer, not that it is *correct*. A 2024 paper claimed fixed formats hurt reasoning, but a rebuttal traced most of that effect to mismatched prompts. Even in the original paper, classification tasks, which is what most of Aegis's tasks are, did better with structure ([Tam et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02442); [dottxt rebuttal](https://blog.dottxt.ai/say-what-you-mean.html)). The docs add three practical points. Put the quote and short explanation before the verdict field, because the model writes from left to right. Compare labels without regard to capitalisation. And if the model refuses, or the answer is cut off by the length limit, the output may not match the format at all.

**Quotes that code checks.** Anthropic's own advice for reducing invented content is to let the model say "I don't know", have it extract word-for-word quotes before doing the task, and withdraw any claim it cannot back with a quote ([Anthropic, Reduce hallucinations](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)). For a CLM, code can go further: check that each quoted passage really appears at the stated position in the stored contract text. A related case shows why every field needs this. In *Concord v. Anthropic*, a lawyer used Claude only to format a citation. The link was right but the title and authors were wrong, and the court struck part of an expert declaration ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/05/15/anthopics_law_firm_blames_claude_hallucinations/)). Checking whether a quote really supports the finding is a separate, harder question. That is the misgrounding problem from the Stanford study, and it needs a person or a separate check.

**Asking more than once.** Self-consistency means asking the same question several times and taking the majority answer. It improved accuracy on reasoning benchmarks by 4 to 18 points ([Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171)). Anthropic suggests the same thing for spotting invented content: run the prompt several times, and treat answers that disagree as a warning ([Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/test-and-evaluate/strengthen-guardrails/reduce-hallucinations)). For lists, a 2026 clinical study ran extraction three times and kept only the items most runs agreed on, which improved accuracy ([arXiv 2604.13502](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.13502)). Asking twice costs twice as much, but there is a simple saving: ask twice, and only ask a third time if the first two disagree ([Certified Self-Consistency](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.17472v2)). Disagreement is useful in its own right. Given the legal-judgement study above, a question that splits the votes is almost always a borderline one, and a lawyer should see it.

**Independent checking, not self-review.** Chain-of-Verification has the model draft an answer, then answer specific checking questions *without seeing its own draft*, and then revise. That more than doubled precision on list questions. The authors stress that the independence matters, because a model that re-reads its own draft tends to repeat its mistakes ([Dhuliawala et al.](https://preview.aclanthology.org/add_acl24_videos/2024.findings-acl.212)). A "critic that reads the draft and fixes it" step therefore gets less benefit than it appears to.

**Treat a cut-off answer as a failure.** When an answer hits the `max_tokens` limit (the maximum length allowed for the model's reply), the API reports `stop_reason: "max_tokens"`. Anthropic says to raise the limit or continue the response, and never to read a cut-off answer as complete ([Anthropic, handling stop reasons](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/handling-stop-reasons)). **Five of the 12 metadata replays were cut off at 4,096 tokens.** That alone is enough to explain much of the task's 50% stability: wherever the cut lands decides which fields exist. Raising the limit treats the symptom. The cure is structural: shorter answers, made of IDs and short values rather than copied text, so the output stays the same size however long the contract is.

**Pin what has been decided.** Because the model will never be perfectly repeatable, an auditable system should not ask it again every time someone opens a contract. Store each AI result with the model version, the prompt version and a fingerprint of the exact input. Show that stored result until something actually changes, and re-run only when someone deliberately asks. This is the same "pinned artifact" rule Aegis already applies to its extracted contract text. It gives consistency, not correctness, so it complements the techniques above rather than replacing them. This recommendation is a reasoned inference; no source measures it.

**Let code make the decisions.** OpenAI describes guardrails as layers, with simple fixed rules alongside model judgements, and a risk rating on every tool so that high-stakes or irreversible actions go to a person ([OpenAI guide](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf)). For Aegis, this means the model proposes a label with evidence. Code then compares the label against the playbook table, applies value and authority thresholds, and gates each workflow step. The model's own confidence never decides anything on its own.

## Keep one real agent, and measure "always right", not "sometimes right"

Anthropic separates *workflows*, where code fixes the sequence of steps, from *agents*, where the model decides its own next steps and tool calls. It advises using an agent only when the steps genuinely cannot be known in advance, because autonomy brings "higher costs, and the potential for compounding errors" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-effective-agents)). OpenAI likewise says that apps where the model doesn't control the flow "are not agents", and that a fixed, deterministic process may be enough ([OpenAI guide](https://cdn.openai.com/business-guides-and-resources/a-practical-guide-to-building-agents.pdf)). By that test, Aegis's split is already correct. Its roughly 34 single calls (labelling, extraction, triage) should stay workflows, and Ask Aegis, which answers open questions across a portfolio, is the one place an agent fits. Systems with several cooperating agents fail often, and mostly because of design and checking problems rather than model weakness. One study catalogued 14 failure modes across more than 1,600 recorded runs ([MAST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.13657)). Anthropic's own multi-agent research system won largely by using about 15 times more tokens ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/multi-agent-research-system)). Splitting a review into a fixed set of small questions captures much of that gain without the model also planning the steps itself.

Ask Aegis should therefore stay an agent, but one whose tools hand back answers code has already worked out. The obligation tools already do this: they return computed `date_state` values, extraction states and a ready-made summary sentence, so the model passes on facts instead of guessing. Every tool that changes something should keep requiring the user's confirmation. Harvey's guidance adds the security side. Treat the matter (in Aegis, the contract or access grant) as a hard boundary for search and for what the model sees. Skip and flag anything the system cannot confirm is in scope. Log every search with its scope ([Harvey](https://www.harvey.ai/blog/agents-and-ethical-walls)). Log blocked access as well, because a block that leaves no record cannot be proven later.

Measuring the right thing matters as much as the design. τ-bench introduced **pass^k**, the chance that *all* k attempts at a task succeed, not just one. GPT-4o's retail success fell to **under 25% at k = 8**, far below its single-attempt score ([τ-bench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12045)). Aegis's "fully stable across 3 replays" measure is close to this and should become a standing part of its testing, alongside accuracy against answers a lawyer has checked. Thomson Reuters describes a mature version of this kind of testing. Attorneys write the test cases and correct answers, other attorneys review them, the whole set is re-run before any model switch, and tests keep running several times a day after release. One test went from 1 out of 5 to passing 99–100% of runs after prompt work ([Thomson Reuters](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/innovation/from-testcase-to-trust-benchmarking-cocounsel-with-scorecard/)). They also warn that automatic scoring which only checks whether an answer *mentions* the right standard will pass answers that never apply it ([Thomson Reuters](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/innovation/when-legal-ai-is-evaluated-like-legal-work-the-results-change/)). Hamel Husain's practical rules fit a small team: use simple pass/fail grades, have one designated domain expert do the labelling, and when one AI grades another, check it against that expert's labels for both kinds of error, using about 30 to 50 examples per label ([Hamel Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)). Agreement between two models proves little. GPT-4o and Claude agreed with each other 79% of the time on legal questions, but with the courts only about 50% ([Blair-Stanek & Van Durme](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.05196v1)).

The professional rules turn all this into an obligation. ABA Formal Opinion 512 makes checking AI output part of a lawyer's duty of competence, with the depth of checking depending on the tool and the task ([ABA Op. 512](https://www.americanbar.org/content/dam/aba/administrative/professional_responsibility/ethics-opinions/aba-formal-opinion-512.pdf)). In *Johnson v. Dunn*, lawyers at a firm *that had an AI policy* were removed from the case and referred to the state bar for fake citations ([EDRM](https://edrm.net/2025/07/when-ai-policies-fail-the-ai-sanctions-in-johnson-v-dunn-and-what-they-mean-for-the-profession/)). A written policy is not a control. The checks have to be part of the software: the source passage shown next to every finding, accept or reject per item, and a permanent record of who accepted what and which model and prompt version produced it.

## The 75way guide is sound, generic, and silent on consistency

The 75way article is a reasonable checklist, but it gives no evidence for its advice. It is written by the company's Chief Marketing Officer, has no publication date and promotes 75way's own services ([75way](https://75way.com/blog/build-ai-agent-for-law-firms)). Its core advice is sound and matches the more detailed sources. It lays out five components: a model, search over approved sources, orchestration of the steps, integrations, and security and oversight. It says to start with the simplest design that works and to measure a baseline before building. It requires lawyer approval for consequential actions, matter-level permissions, audit logs that leave out unnecessary confidential content, re-running tests after every change, and treating text inside documents as untrusted, since a document can contain instructions aimed at the AI (prompt injection). It is honest that search over approved sources "does not guarantee accuracy". Its best single idea is to measure value as time saved *after* attorney review, not raw generation speed.

The article falls short exactly where Aegis's problem lies. **It never mentions that the same input can give different answers.** It says nothing about asking twice, pinning results, checking quotes or splitting open tasks into closed ones. It recommends tests but not how to build an answer key or who should write it. It does not name ethical walls or say permissions should come from the document and conflicts systems. It gives no failure data or court cases. Its cost ranges ($15k–$50k for one focused workflow, $50k–$150k+ for a multi-system build) have no source. Its list of tools reads more like search-engine filler than a reasoned choice. It also uses "agent" loosely for what Anthropic and OpenAI would call workflows. If Aegis followed it literally, it might end up with more agents than it needs. Treat it as a reminder of the basics, not a design source. The Harvey, Thomson Reuters and Ironclad material above goes much further on the same topics.

Some things should stay as they are, because the research supports them. The deterministic document reader as the authoritative list of clauses, and the rule that extracted text is never re-read, are the strongest pieces of the current design. Clause labelling's "clause number in, label out, code attaches the text" pattern is exactly what the long-document research recommends. So is computing obligation dates in code rather than asking the model for calendar dates. Intake triage, email triage and portfolio filtering are already 100% stable and should not be touched. Temperature 0 is harmless and worth keeping, provided nobody mistakes it for a guarantee. Plain-language failure reasons, no automatic re-run after a failed AI call, and requiring confirmation before Ask Aegis takes an action all match OpenAI's advice on risky tools. Delivering AI edits as tracked suggestions tied to their issue is how Ironclad, Spellbook and ContractMatrix put a lawyer in control. Ask Aegis should remain the single real agent. Do not turn the fixed tasks into agents, and do not add a separate "critic" agent that reviews another agent's draft.

Three caveats apply. Splitting review into more questions finds more but adds some false alarms ([UFPE](https://repositorio.ufpe.br/handle/123456789/58332?locale=en)), so the quote check and the agreement filter are part of the fix, not optional extras. Asking twice roughly doubles the cost of those steps. Each per-rule question is short, which offsets part of that, and asking a third time only on disagreement limits it further. And because no published study measures run-to-run consistency for contract review specifically, the replay harness, re-run after each change, is the only proof that a change worked for Aegis.

## Conclusion

The measurements change what "making Aegis's AI reliable" means. The model is not the problem: Claude was the most stable model in the legal-judgement study, and Aegis's closed tasks are already perfectly repeatable. The problem is the two places where Aegis asks the model to define the scope of its own answer. Once code decides what is being asked about (each clause, each rule, each field) and what an answer means, the model's remaining variation shrinks to the genuinely borderline calls. Those are exactly the calls a lawyer should see. Instability stops being hidden noise and becomes a way to send the right questions to a person.

For legal work, the goal is not identical text on every run, which no hosted model can deliver. The goal is a result that is pinned and traceable: each finding tied to checkable text, decided by visible rules, accepted by a named person, and tested repeatedly against a lawyer's answer key. The 75way guide, like most practitioner guides, stops before this point. Aegis already has the deterministic base the research recommends. The work now is to put playbook review and obligation extraction on that base, then keep measuring repeatability as a standing quality bar, not a one-off experiment.
